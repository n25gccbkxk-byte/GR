Module 2 · The Passage & Six Question Types

Reading Comprehension

One RC section. Four passages. Each one is roughly 450–500 words of legal scholarship, science, humanities, or social science, followed by 5–8 questions. The passage we're using today is a Kris Jenner business memo. The skills you'll use on it are the same skills you'll use on a passage about constitutional law.

The five things to mark up

Before you touch a question, annotate the passage for:

  • Main point. One sentence. What is the author trying to convince me of?
  • Author's attitude. Pro, anti, neutral, mixed? Look for tone words: "unfortunately," "remarkable," "merely."
  • Structure. Each paragraph's function: introduces the issue, presents an objection, rebuts, concludes.
  • Viewpoints. Anyone who is not the author — "Critics argue," "Some scholars contend."
  • Pivot words. But, however, yet, although, surprisingly. These flag where the argument turns.

The Passage

(1) To: the family. From: Kris. Re: the next decade.

(5) For nearly twenty years our brand has rested on the premise that proximity sells — that the closer the audience feels to our daily lives, the more they will buy, watch, and post. Critics of this approach argue it is exhausting, both for the family and the audience, and they point to declining engagement on the long-form series as evidence of fatigue. Some have urged us to retreat into ventures that do not require us to be on camera at all.

(15) The critics are not wrong about the fatigue, but they have misdiagnosed its cause. Audiences have not tired of proximity itself; they have tired of manufactured proximity — the heavily produced confessional, the storyline plotted in a writers' room, the scripted reconciliation. What the data show, in contrast, is sustained appetite for the unguarded moment: a Sunday-night Instagram story, a podcast aside, a candid response to a paparazzi question.

(25) I therefore propose a structural shift. Beginning next quarter, the family should reduce its commitment to scripted long-form programming by half and reinvest the freed time into platforms that reward unmediated presence. This is not a retreat from the camera; it is a recommitment to the original proposition, on terms the audience now prefers.

The question types — what each is really asking

Question Type 1

Main Point

"Which one of the following most accurately expresses the main point of the passage?"

Correct main point The family should shift away from heavily produced long-form programming and into platforms that reward unmediated presence, because audience fatigue is with manufactured proximity rather than proximity itself.
Trap answers will (a) be true but narrow ("audiences are tired"); (b) match the critics' view, not the author's ("retreat from camera"); (c) overreach ("scripted TV is dead"). The main point must cover the whole passage and reflect the author's conclusion.
Question Type 2

Primary Purpose

"The primary purpose of the passage is to..."

Correct purpose Propose a strategic shift and defend it against a competing diagnosis.
Purpose answers begin with verbs: argue, propose, refute, reconcile, explain, describe, evaluate. Match the verb to what the author is doing — not what the passage is about. Kris isn't describing the industry; she is proposing a course of action.
Question Type 3

Author's Attitude / Tone

"The author's attitude toward critics of the proximity-sells strategy can best be described as..."

Correct attitude Partial agreement combined with disagreement on causation. Kris concedes the critics see real fatigue ("not wrong"), but asserts they misidentify its source.
Watch for hedged tone words: "not wrong, but..." flags partial agreement. Wrong answers will be too extreme ("dismissive," "wholly persuaded") or wrong direction (negative when the author is mixed).
Question Type 4

Specific Detail

"According to the passage, the critics' explanation for declining engagement is..."

Correct answer Audience and family fatigue with the proximity-based approach (lines 8–11).
Detail questions reward going back to the passage. Don't answer from memory. The right answer will paraphrase the text — if a choice uses a word verbatim from the passage but in a different context, that's a trap.
Question Type 5

Inference

"It can most reasonably be inferred from the passage that the author would agree with which of the following?"

Defensible inference A podcast aside is more valuable to the brand than an additional scripted episode would be.
RC inferences are conservative. The author explicitly praises "unmediated presence" and cites podcast asides as examples, while proposing to cut scripted long-form. The comparative inference follows directly. Bigger leaps ("scripted TV will die out") are not supported.
Question Type 6

Structure / Function

"The function of the second paragraph is to..."

Correct function Concede a factual observation made by critics while reframing its cause, in service of the proposal that follows in paragraph three.
Function answers describe a paragraph's job in the argument, not its content. Don't summarize — classify. Common functions: introduce the issue, present the opposing view, offer a concession, rebut, illustrate, conclude.

Comparative passages

One of the four RC passages each test is presented as two short passages instead of one long one. Treat them as two reality stars in conversation. Ask: where do they agree? Where do they disagree? Is one broader than the other? A typical comparative question is: "Both passages would agree with..." or "The author of Passage B would most likely respond to the conclusion of Passage A by..."

"If Kim wrote Passage A and Khloé wrote Passage B, your job is to diagram exactly where the sisters agree and exactly where they don't."

Timing strategy

You have ~8.75 minutes per passage. A workable split:

  • 3 minutes reading and annotating the passage.
  • 5–6 minutes on the questions, going back to the text on every detail or inference question.

Do not try to memorize the passage. Memorize where things live — "the critics' view is in paragraph 1," "the proposal is in paragraph 3" — so you can find evidence fast.
