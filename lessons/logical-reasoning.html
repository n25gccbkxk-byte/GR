Module 1 · 10 Question Types

Logical Reasoning

Two of the four scored LSAT sections are Logical Reasoning. Each question hands you a one-paragraph argument (the stimulus), asks you something about it (the question stem), and gives you five answer choices. Below: the ten question types you'll see, each taught with a scene you already know by heart.

Before you touch a single answer choice, do three things on every LR question:

  1. Read the question stem first. It tells you what job the stimulus is doing.
  2. Identify the conclusion. Ask: what is the author trying to get me to believe?
  3. Find the premises. The "because" sentences supporting the conclusion.

"An LSAT stimulus is just a confessional. Premises are the receipts, the conclusion is the read."

1. Necessary Assumption

A necessary assumption is an unstated premise the argument absolutely must have to work. Use the negation test: if negating an answer choice destroys the argument, that's your answer.

RHOA Scene

Kandi vs. Phaedra at the reunion

Kandi argues: "Phaedra has to be lying about being at the studio that night, because Apollo told me she wasn't there."

Conclusion Phaedra is lying.

Premise Apollo says Phaedra wasn't at the studio.
Necessary assumption: Apollo is telling the truth.
Negation test: "Apollo is not telling the truth." Kandi's whole argument collapses. That's why the assumption is required.

2. Sufficient Assumption

A sufficient assumption is the extra premise that, if added to the stimulus, would guarantee the conclusion. Bigger than necessary — it doesn't just keep the argument alive, it closes the deal.

Kardashians Scene

Kris closing the deal at a pitch meeting

Kris argues: "Khloé's denim line will succeed because the Kardashian name attaches to it."

Conclusion The line will succeed.

Premise The Kardashian name is attached.
Sufficient assumption: "Every product the Kardashian name attaches to succeeds." Add that universal premise and the conclusion is locked. Necessary assumptions are smaller ("the name has some impact"); sufficient ones are big enough to force the conclusion.

3. Strengthen

A strengthen question asks which new fact would make the conclusion more likely. You don't need to prove it — just nudge the scale.

RHOA Scene

Kenya defending her peach

Kenya argues: "My storyline drives the season's ratings, so Bravo should give me top billing."

Strengthener: "Internal Bravo viewership data shows episodes centered on Kenya's storyline pull 22% more viewers than the season average." That's a fact that makes her conclusion more credible — it doesn't prove top billing is owed, but it stacks the deck. Trap answers will be irrelevant (Kenya is well-dressed), too strong (Kenya should host the reunion), or about the wrong party.

4. Weaken

A weaken question asks which fact would undermine the conclusion. Look for an alternate explanation, a counterexample, or a missing causal link.

Kardashians Scene

Kim claims her shapewear launch broke the internet

Kim argues: "The launch was a record-breaking success because SKIMS sold out within an hour. That proves the marketing campaign worked."

Weakener: "Only 800 units were stocked, far below the typical launch volume." Now the sellout has an alternate explanation (low supply, not marketing). The causal claim — campaign → sellout — collapses without the campaign needing to be touched directly.

5. Flaw in the Reasoning

A flaw question asks you to name what's wrong with the argument. Memorize the common flaw families: correlation/causation, ad hominem, equivocation, circular reasoning, false dichotomy, sampling, percent vs. number, part vs. whole.

RHOA Scene

Porsha analyzing Marlo

Porsha says: "Marlo doesn't like me, so Marlo must be jealous of me."

Flaw: Porsha treats "dislike" as if its only possible cause is "jealousy." The flaw name on the LSAT: fails to consider alternative explanations. Marlo could dislike Porsha for any number of reasons unrelated to jealousy.

6. Method of Reasoning

A method question asks how the argument works — not whether it's right. The answer describes the structural move (analogy, counterexample, conditional chain, etc.).

Kardashians Scene

Kourtney making a lifestyle case

Kourtney argues: "My grandmother lived to 95 eating organic food and avoiding processed sugar. Therefore, eating organic and avoiding processed sugar is the path to a long life."

Method: Generalizes from a single example to a universal claim. On the LSAT this is often phrased: "draws a general conclusion from a single instance." The correct method answer is describing the move — not naming the flaw.

7. Main Point / Main Conclusion

A main point question asks: what is the author ultimately trying to get the reader to accept? Watch for sub-conclusions (they support the main point but aren't it).

RHOA Scene

NeNe addressing the group

NeNe says: "I've been on this show for ten years. I know what the fans want. Cynthia thinks the trip should be to Anguilla, but Anguilla is overdone. We should go to Greece. Greece has the views, the boats, and the drama."

Main point: "We should go to Greece." Everything else (her tenure, fan expertise, Anguilla critique) is a premise supporting that prescriptive conclusion. A trap answer would isolate a premise ("Anguilla is overdone").

8. Inference (Must Be True)

An inference question gives you a set of facts and asks what must follow. Don't bring in outside knowledge. The correct answer is rarely earth-shattering — it's the boring, airtight one.

Kardashians Scene

Schedule conflict at Kris's house

Given: "All of Kris's daughters were at the family dinner. Kylie was not at the family dinner. Kim and Kourtney both attended."

Must be true: Kylie is not one of Kris's daughters. The facts force it — if "all daughters" attended and Kylie didn't, Kylie isn't a daughter (in this stimulus's universe). Resist the urge to import what you know about the family — on inference questions, the four corners of the stimulus are all you have.

9. Principle (Identify or Apply)

A principle question gives you a general rule and a specific scenario, and asks you to match them. Two flavors: which principle justifies the conclusion (rule → case), or which case best illustrates the principle (case → rule).

RHOA Scene

Andy Cohen lays down a rule

Principle: "A housewife should be held accountable for a comment only if she made the comment publicly and did not retract it before the reunion."

Scenario: Drew made a cutting comment in a confessional that aired on TV. She has not retracted it.

Application: Drew should be held accountable. The principle's two conditions (public + not retracted) are both satisfied. Wrong-answer traps will involve scenarios where one condition fails — a private text, or a retraction issued the day before the reunion.

10. Parallel Reasoning (and Parallel Flaw)

A parallel reasoning question asks: which answer choice has the same logical structure as the stimulus? Match the shape, not the subject matter.

Kardashians Scene

Khloé builds a conditional chain

"If Tristan shows up, then the dinner gets awkward. If the dinner gets awkward, Kris will leave early. So if Tristan shows up, Kris will leave early."

Structure: If A then B. If B then C. Therefore, if A then C. (Hypothetical syllogism.) A parallel-reasoning answer would have the same A→B, B→C, A→C shape with completely different content — say, a chain about contracts, weather, or commute times. Subject matter is noise; structure is the signal.

11. Resolve the Paradox

A paradox question presents two facts that seem to contradict. You're asked to find the answer that explains why both can be true at the same time.

RHOA Scene

The reunion that won't end

"Kenya and Marlo had a screaming match on the reunion couch. Yet two weeks later, they were filmed brunching together, laughing."

Resolution: The fight was filmed months ago and the brunch happened after a producer-mediated peace offering. Both facts are true once you supply the missing piece. On test day, paradox answers will look like: "Resolves the apparent contradiction by introducing X." Eliminate any answer that only restates one side of the paradox.

Putting it together

Every LR question is a reality TV scene with the editing on full blast. Your job is to identify what kind of scene it is (question type), find the line that proves the case (conclusion + premises), and pick the choice that answers the producer's question exactly — not almost, not in spirit, exactly.
