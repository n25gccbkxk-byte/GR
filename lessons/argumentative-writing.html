Module 3 · The Writing Sample

Argumentative Writing

The LSAT Writing sample is taken separately from the multiple-choice test, on your own computer with a remote proctor. It's unscored, but every law school you apply to reads it. Since 2024 it has used the Argumentative Writing format: a debatable issue, two perspectives, three to four pieces of evidence, and 50 minutes to write your essay. The good news: this is a reunion couch in essay form.

The format, decoded

  • 15 minutes pre-writing analysis (untimed reading + brainstorming).
  • 50 minutes to write.
  • You'll see a debate topic, two positions, and a list of evidentiary considerations.
  • You pick a side. You defend it. You address the counterposition. You use the evidence provided.

Think of it as a reunion

Imagine the topic is: Should the family relocate filming from Calabasas to Aspen? Position A: stay in Calabasas. Position B: move to Aspen. You sit on the reunion couch. You pick a side. You say why. You let the other side speak — and then you explain why they're still wrong.

"On the reunion couch you don't just say your side — you say the other side's side, and then you say why your side wins. That is LSAT Argumentative Writing in one sentence."

The four-part structure that always works

Paragraph 1

Stake the claim

Two to three sentences. Name the issue. State your position. Preview your two best reasons. Don't try to be clever; clarity scores.

Example opener The family should relocate filming to Aspen. Aspen offers both a fresh visual environment that revitalizes the show and a lower production cost than Calabasas. While remaining in Calabasas preserves continuity with the existing audience, the benefits of relocating outweigh that concern.
Paragraph 2

Develop your strongest reason

One reason. Backed with two pieces of evidence from the prompt. Explain why the evidence supports your claim — don't just quote it.

Move Claim → Evidence → Reasoning that connects them. Repeat.
Paragraph 3

Address the counterposition fairly — then defeat it

This is the paragraph that separates a 3-rated essay from a 6-rated one. State the strongest version of the opposing view. Concede what's true about it. Then explain why your position still wins.

Move "Critics will argue..." → "They are correct that..." → "However, this consideration is outweighed by..."
The reunion couch test: if you can imagine the other housewife reading your paragraph and saying "she actually heard me," you've done concession right.
Paragraph 4

Land it

One short paragraph. Restate your position in fresh words. Reference the trade-off you've acknowledged. Do not introduce new arguments — new arguments in a conclusion are the LSAT equivalent of bringing receipts after the reunion has wrapped.

What the readers reward

  • A clear position. No "both sides have merit" mush.
  • Engagement with the evidence provided. Use it — don't invent your own facts.
  • An honest treatment of the counterposition. Strawmanning lowers your score.
  • Logical transitions. "Furthermore," "however," "as a result" — signal the structure.
  • Clean mechanics. Typos and grammar slips don't help, even on an unscored sample.

What the readers penalize

  • Picking neither side. Or picking both.
  • Ignoring the counterposition entirely.
  • Importing personal stories instead of using the evidence the prompt gave you.
  • Padding. A tight 350-word essay beats a sprawling 700-word one.

A 15-minute pre-write you can actually do

  1. 3 min: Read the prompt and evidence twice.
  2. 2 min: Sort the evidence into "supports A" / "supports B" / "either" piles.
  3. 3 min: Pick the side with the heavier pile (not the side you "agree with").
  4. 4 min: Outline four paragraphs: claim, best reason, counterposition + rebuttal, close.
  5. 3 min: Draft your thesis sentence and your concession sentence. The rest will flow.

Worked example, in miniature

Sample prompt (simplified)

Should the show film a wedding episode this season?

Position A: Yes — weddings drive a ratings spike and generate brand partnership revenue.

Position B: No — weddings invite premature exposure and have historically preceded high-profile splits.

Evidence: (1) The last three wedding episodes averaged 38% higher viewership than the season average. (2) Brand sponsorships during wedding episodes generated record revenue. (3) Two of the three couples whose weddings were televised divorced within four years. (4) The current couple has been together for eighteen months.

Sample essay (condensed)

The family should film the wedding

The family should film the wedding episode. The ratings and revenue benefits are substantial and proven, while the chief objection — that televised weddings precede splits — rests on a small and not clearly causal sample.

The evidence on commercial upside is decisive. The last three wedding episodes pulled 38% above the season average, and brand sponsorships during those episodes set revenue records. Together, these two facts establish that a wedding episode reliably moves both audience and dollars in the right direction — a result not matched by ordinary season programming.

Critics will respond that two of three televised weddings ended in divorce within four years, and that the current couple has been together for only eighteen months. The concern is fair: a ratings spike is not worth a marriage. But correlation here is not causation; the data given do not show televising caused the splits, and a sample of three is not a pattern. The eighteen-month tenure is on the short side, but the prompt provides no evidence that this duration predicts divorce.

The verifiable, repeatable benefits of filming the wedding outweigh a speculative, sample-limited risk. The family should proceed.

That's the whole game. Pick a side, defend it with the evidence on the page, treat the other side fairly, and land the plane in four paragraphs.
